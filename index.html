Predicting High-Impact Power Outages

A data science lifecycle project studying which major U.S. power outages affect unusually many customers.

Introduction

This project analyzes major power outage events in the continental United States from 2000 to 2016. I focus on customer impact: which outages affected unusually many customers, and which location, climate, cause, and infrastructure characteristics are associated with those outages.

The main question is: What factors are associated with major power outages that affect unusually many customers? For prediction, I define HIGH_IMPACT as whether CUSTOMERS.AFFECTED is above the median observed customer impact.

Data Cleaning and Exploratory Data Analysis

The raw data is an Excel workbook. The first five rows are metadata, so I load the sheet with header=5. The first loaded row contains units rather than an outage observation, so I remove it by keeping rows with numeric YEAR values. I also combine OUTAGE.START.DATE with OUTAGE.START.TIME into OUTAGE.START, and combine the restoration date and time columns into OUTAGE.RESTORATION.

Severe weather is the most common cause category by a wide margin, followed by intentional attacks.

Outages are most common in summer months, especially June and July.

Severe weather outages have the highest median customer impact, which motivates using cause category in later modeling.

California and Texas have the largest number of recorded major outages in this dataset.

Assessment of Missingness

I chose CUSTOMERS.AFFECTED for missingness analysis because it is my main severity variable and is missing in about 28.9% of rows. The missingness appears related to outage cause: severe weather outages are rarely missing customer impact, while fuel supply emergencies and public appeals are often missing it.

Missingness test: I used total variation distance to compare the distribution of CAUSE.CATEGORY for rows where CUSTOMERS.AFFECTED was missing versus observed. The permutation test produced a very small p-value, so I found evidence that missingness depends on cause category.

I also tested whether missingness depends on CLIMATE.CATEGORY. That p-value was large, so I did not find evidence that missingness depends on climate category.

Hypothesis Testing

I tested whether severe-weather outages affect more customers than non-severe-weather outages.

Null HypothesisSevere-weather and non-severe-weather outages have the same distribution of CUSTOMERS.AFFECTED.
Alternative HypothesisSevere-weather outages tend to affect more customers than non-severe-weather outages.
Test StatisticDifference in mean customers affected: severe weather minus non-severe weather.
ResultThe observed difference was about 131,616 customers. The permutation test p-value was approximately 0.0000, so I rejected the null hypothesis.

Prediction Problem and Models

The prediction task is binary classification: predict HIGH_IMPACT, where an outage is high-impact if CUSTOMERS.AFFECTED is above the median observed customer impact.

Baseline Model

The baseline logistic regression model uses only MONTH and CLIMATE.CATEGORY, both one-hot encoded. It is intentionally simple and uses information available near the start of an outage.

Final Model

The final model adds cause, state, NERC region, climate region, climate anomaly, population, electricity sales, electricity prices, and engineered features such as season, log population, log total customers, and customers per person. Hyperparameters are tuned with cross-validation.

Fairness Analysis

I compared accuracy for outages during warm climate periods against outages during non-warm climate periods. The observed absolute difference in accuracy was about 0.085, and the permutation test p-value was about 0.2312.

At the 0.05 significance level, I failed to reject the null hypothesis. I did not find strong evidence that the final model is unfair between warm and non-warm climate-category outages using accuracy parity.
